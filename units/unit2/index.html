🚧 Unit 2 • Crime Scene Investigation & Evidence Collection

The scene tells a story — but only if we protect it.

In this unit, students move from “noticing details” to working like a crime-scene team. They learn Locard’s Principle, evidence types, scene security, documentation, searching, packaging, chain of custody, and the logic behind reconstructing what may have happened.

Unit Goals

Students learn the careful procedures that make forensic evidence useful, trustworthy, and scientifically defensible.

🔁

Explain Locard’s Principle

Students connect the idea of transfer evidence to real crime-scene decisions and contamination risks.

📷

Document Before Touching

Students learn why notes, photos, videos, and sketches must happen before evidence is moved.

📦

Protect Evidence Integrity

Students practice packaging, labeling, and chain-of-custody thinking so evidence can be trusted later.

General Notes

Use these as the main student-facing note sections. They are intentionally chunked so you can teach them in any order.

🎯
Goal of a crime-scene investigation
Recognize, document, collect, preserve, and interpret evidence while minimizing contamination and protecting the rights of everyone involved.
🔁
Locard’s Principle of Exchange
When two people or objects come into contact, material can transfer between them. That transfer can include hair, fibers, soil, glass, fingerprints, blood, paint, pollen, or digital traces.
⚠️
Contamination risk
Evidence can be added, removed, moved, damaged, or mislabeled. A sloppy scene can make strong evidence weak.
🧬
Physical evidence
Any object, material, mark, or substance that can help establish facts about a crime.
👁️
Direct evidence
Evidence that directly supports a fact, such as a credible eyewitness statement or video of the event.
🧩
Circumstantial evidence
Evidence that requires reasoning to connect it to a conclusion, such as fingerprints on a window, shoeprints near an exit, or glass fragments on clothing.
🚓
First responder
Secures the scene, checks safety, protects life, limits access, and starts the scene log.
📸
Photographer / videographer
Documents the scene from wide, medium, and close-up views before evidence is moved.
📝
Recorder / sketch artist / evidence collector
Records details, maps evidence positions, collects items properly, and maintains labels and custody.
🏷️
Packaging evidence
Evidence must be packaged separately when possible, labeled clearly, sealed, and stored in a way that prevents loss, damage, and cross-contamination.
🔐
Chain of custody
The documented record of who collected, handled, transferred, tested, and stored evidence from the scene to the courtroom.
🧠
Reconstruction
Investigators use evidence patterns, witness statements, timelines, and physical clues to build a testable explanation of what happened.

The Seven S’s of Crime-Scene Investigation

This sequence gives students a simple mental model for crime-scene work.

1SecureProtect life, preserve evidence, restrict access.
2SeparateKeep witnesses apart to reduce influence.
3ScanObserve the whole scene and identify hazards.
4SeePhotograph and document before moving items.
5SketchMap evidence and important scene features.
6SearchUse a systematic pattern to locate evidence.
7Secure & CollectPackage, label, seal, and log each item.
Teacher move: Have students defend which “S” is easiest to rush and which one causes the biggest problems if done poorly.

Scene Procedure Check

Click each question to reveal the reasoning.

Because uncontrolled people, weather, movement, and curiosity can contaminate, damage, or remove evidence before it is documented.
Witnesses can unintentionally influence each other. Separating them preserves independent accounts.
Photos preserve the original position and context of evidence. Once something is moved, the scene cannot truly be put back.
Separate packaging prevents transfer between items and helps protect trace evidence from being mixed or lost.
